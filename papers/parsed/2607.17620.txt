Abstract
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) makes finetuning large language models cheaper by adding to each
weight matrix a trainable low-rank update parameterized as the product of two matrices. These matrices
are usually trained with Adam, which treats them as a single flat vector of parameters and ignores
both the matrix and product structure of LoRA. Applying a matrix-aware optimizer such as Muon to
each factor does not consistently improve over Adam, and neither do the product-aware Muon variants
proposed in concurrent works. To realize consistent gains, we introduce PoLoRA, a Preconditioned
Orthogonalized LoRA optimizer built from three ingredients: a product-aware spectral update direction,
curvature preconditioning derived from controlling the per-sample loss change, and a magnitude rule
that controls the sizes of both the factor and merged updates. We evaluate PoLoRA on instruction-tuning
datasets for code and math across models from 1B to 8B parameters, and find that it reaches the final
held-out loss achieved by tuned Adam in 1.2–1.7 times fewer steps, while adding at most 3% per-step
overhead. Compared to Adam, PoLoRA is also less sensitive to the learning rate, and its optimal learning
rate is stable across ranks.

Introduction
Deployment of large-scale pretrained models increasingly depends on adapting a general model to many
specialized tasks [7]. Finetuning a separate full copy for each task is expensive, because training must hold
gradients and optimizer state for the whole model, and deploying each task means storing and serving
multiple full-sized models. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA [22]) avoids both issues by freezing the base weight
W0 ∈Rdout×din and adding a small low-rank update ∆W,
W = W0 + ∆W,

to each layer, with α a scaling parameter and r the rank. With only the adapter (B, A) trained, finetuning
can run on nearly the same hardware layouts as inference, and the shared base lets one server serve many
adapters at once [11, 37, 46].
The standard optimization recipe for LoRA applies Adam [31] to the two factors A and B, treating all
the parameters as a single flat vector and ignoring any matrix structure. In language-model pretraining,
matrix-aware optimizers such as Muon [26], Scion [43], and Shampoo [18] treat each weight as a matrix
and outperform Adam [18, 38, 44, 48, 54]. Muon in particular and variants such as NorMuon [35] have set
records for training small language models [25, 29] and have been adopted in recent large-scale training
runs [14, 16].
It is natural to ask whether similar gains can carry over to LoRA. We found, however, that applying
Muon to each factor does not improve over Adam. Part of the problem is structural: the model sees the

To obtain a consistent speedup over Adam, we introduce PoLoRA, a steepest descent method whose
constraint is motivated by controlling the change in the per-sample loss, and whose solution is an or-
thogonalized update preconditioned by a curvature estimate. We then develop a product-aware version
of this update for LoRA, imposing the constraint on the merged update rather than on each factor sepa-
rately. A separate magnitude rule sets the sizes of the factor updates, which the Product Muon step leaves
uncontrolled, while retaining its bound on the spectral norm of the merged update.
Across base models, datasets, and adapter ranks, PoLoRA consistently improves training efficiency over
Adam (Tables 1 and 2), and it outperforms matrix-aware LoRA baselines on Llama-3.2-1B finetuned on
math (Figure 1). It reaches the final held-out loss of tuned Adam in 1.2–1.7× fewer steps, while adding
at most 3% per-step overhead. Removing the curvature preconditioning and the magnitude rule in turn
reduces PoLoRA to Product Muon, and each component accounts for about half of the speedup gap. Finally,
we observe that PoLoRA is easier to tune than Adam: its optimal learning rate is stable across ranks, and
performance is less sensitive to the learning rate.

PoLoRA: Preconditioned Orthogonalized LoRA
To derive the PoLoRA update, we first recall in Section 2.1 how Muon arises from constrained steepest
descent [13]. Its update is computed by a linear minimization oracle (LMO) [43], which minimizes the
linearized loss subject to a spectral-norm bound. In Section 2.2 we make this LMO product-aware, which
yields Product Muon. In Section 2.3 we obtain the PoLoRA update direction by replacing the spectral-norm
constraint with a bound on the change in the per-sample loss and making the resulting LMO product-aware
for LoRA. The LMO provides the direction but not the size of the update, which we set by a separate
rescaling that controls both the per-factor and merged-weight updates. All proofs are in Appendix B.
Throughout, whenever a vector or matrix is divided by its norm, we set X/∥X∥:= 0 at X = 0.

• Averaging. Momentum with look-ahead replaces each factor gradient with an EMA estimate of its full-

• Diagonal curvature. To reduce overhead, we take P and Q diagonal, as in Adafactor [49] (line 2), and

pendix E.1). We use Gram Newton–Schulz iterations with PolarExpress coefficients [1] to compute the

Experiments
We now empirically evaluate PoLoRA, comparing it to several baselines in Section 3.2, investigating which
design choices of PoLoRA are responsible for its efficacy in Section 3.3, and testing its behavior across ranks
and on different data in Section 3.4.
3.1
Setup
Comparison.
For every model, dataset, and rank, we only vary the optimizer and learning rate. All
methods use LoRA on every linear layer except the output layer, with the standard initialization B = 0 and
random A [22]. For each optimizer, we sweep the learning rate under a constant schedule with no weight
decay and select the run with the lowest held-out loss.
The main comparisons finetune OLMo-2-1B [41], Llama-3.2-1B [17], Qwen2.5-1.5B [55], and Llama-3-8B
on code (OpenCoder [23]) and math (OpenMathInstruct-2 [52]) datasets, using rank r = 256 unless stated
otherwise. Additional experiments in Section 3.4 vary the rank as well as change the finetuning dataset to
a low-resource language. Appendix A gives the full experimental configurations.
Baselines.
We compare PoLoRA (Section 2) to the following optimizers:
• Adam [31] — our baseline, applied independently to the two factors A and B.
• Muon [26] — the solution of the spectral LMO (2), applied independently to each factor.
• iMuon [34] — a product-aware spectral LMO (26) applied to momentum in the product space.
• LoRA-RITE [57] — an adaptive LoRA optimizer that preconditions factor gradients in a transformation-
invariant basis.

3.2
Computational Efficiency
We score each optimizer by its steps-to-Adam, the number of steps its tuned run needs to match the final
loss of tuned Adam. The step speedup is then the ratio of Adam’s total step count to the steps-to-Adam.
Across our experiments, PoLoRA reaches this loss in 1.2–1.7× fewer steps (Table 1). On Llama-3.2-1B
finetuned on math, PoLoRA also outperforms Muon, iMuon, and LoRA-RITE, none of which improves over
Adam (Figure 1). Additional learning curves can be found in Appendix H.
Wall-clock.
The optimizer FLOPs of each PoLoRA step are independent of the batch size, while the
model forward and backward passes scale with it (Appendix F). Thus the relative per-step time shrinks
as the batch grows. At our batch size (Appendix A) it adds at most 3% wall-clock overhead compared to
Adam on all four base models (Table 1), and the step speedup carries to wall-clock almost unchanged.
3.3
Component Ablation
The essential ingredients in PoLoRA can be decomposed into three components:
• Product-awareness. The direction LMO (4) uses the structure of the merged product BA as opposed
to treating A and B as independent matrices.
• Curvature. Preconditioning the gradient by an adaptive curvature estimate makes the update account
for how different directions change the loss (11).
• Magnitude. The sizes of the factor steps and the merged update are both controlled (21).
Removing the curvature preconditioning (P and Q set to identity) and the magnitude control (τ = η/2 in
(16)) reduces PoLoRA to Product Muon (6). On Llama-3.2-1B finetuned on math (Figure 2), Product Muon
has no speedup over Adam, and the curvature and magnitude components each account for about half the
speedup gap between Product Muon and PoLoRA.

that the speedup over Adam is larger on the Bengali dataset (Aya [51]) than on code. This is consistent with
the advantage of PoLoRA growing as finetuning must move the model further from its pretraining data.

scale with α/√r to avoid the rank-dependent shrinkage that makes high-rank adapters hard to train,
and µA [12] studies how these hyperparameters should scale with rank to keep the optimal learning rate
stable. In contrast, PoLoRA keeps these scalar hyperparameters fixed and instead changes the update itself,
computing the factor update directions in a curvature-aware metric on the merged product BA rather than
rescaling a coordinatewise Adam step.
Matrix-aware updates.
Recently, optimizers that operate at the matrix level, rather than treating
parameters as a flattened vector, have attracted attention for their gains over Adam [38, 44, 54]. Muon [26]
solves the spectral-norm LMO for a weight matrix, producing an update equal to the matrix sign of
the momentum gradient [1] (Lemma 2). Shampoo [18], SOAP [53], and KL-Shampoo [36] estimate the
gradient curvature as Kronecker-factored second moments. Mousse [59] uses such a second moment
estimate to whiten the gradient before a matrix-sign step. PoLoRA likewise applies a matrix-sign step to a
preconditioned gradient, with a metric derived from controlling the per-sample loss change across the batch
(Section 2.3). We carry this update from the full weight matrix to the LoRA product and add magnitude
control, which removes the need for learning-rate grafting [50, 59]. We adapt the KL-Shampoo estimator to
LoRA, fitting the preconditioners from the factor gradients rather than the weight gradient (Appendix D).
Product-invariant LoRA optimizers.
The learned update BA is a product of two matrices, so it is
determined only up to the change of basis (A, B) 7→(NA, BN−1) for invertible N. Since Adam is not
invariant to this transformation, a line of work has proposed LoRA optimizers that are. LoRA-RITE removes
the basis dependence with an adaptive preconditioner [57], while Riemannion optimizes BA directly on
the fixed-rank matrix manifold [6]. iMuon, LoRA-Muon, and Compositional Muon derive invariant updates
from spectral-norm LMOs [10, 30, 34]. As discussed in Appendix C, the momentum-free LMO updates of
LoRA-RITE, LoRA-Muon, and Compositional Muon’s half-split rule all coincide with (4), as does iMuon’s
intrinsic LMO. PoLoRA does not satisfy this invariance, but it outperforms iMuon in our experiments
(Section 3.2), suggesting that invariance is not the limiting factor in this setting.

Magnitude control for low-rank products.
Product Muon (4) bounds ∥B∆A∥2 and ∥∆BA∥2 but
not the factor steps ∥∆A∥2 and ∥∆B∥2. As a result, when A or B has small singular values, the updates
∆A and ∆B can be large enough to destabilize training. Spectron [24] and QuacK [2] explicitly control
the factor steps by scaling them with the factor norms. Spectron rescales both steps to spectral norm
η/(∥A∥2 +∥B∥2 +1), and QuacK scales each factor’s learning rate by the inverse norm of the other. Similar
to Spectron, PoLoRA rescales both factor steps to a common spectral norm ρ = η/(∥A∥2 + ∥B∥2). With
the +1 in its denominator, Spectron’s bound also covers the quadratic term ∆B∆A, which PoLoRA drops
(Remark 1 verifies that restoring it leaves the bound essentially unchanged).
5
Conclusion
PoLoRA takes a different route to optimizing LoRA adapters: it asks what a step does to the merged product
BA and to the per-sample losses, rather than treating the two LoRA factors as unrelated parameter matrices.
This view leads to a practical optimizer that combines a product-aware spectral direction, lightweight
curvature preconditioning, and explicit control of factor-step magnitudes. In our experiments this makes
matrix-aware LoRA optimization consistently faster than Adam, and the optimal learning rate transfers
across ranks.
The method is still built on approximations. For efficiency the preconditioners are diagonal, and the
metric they induce is a Kronecker-factored approximation of the per-sample second moment. The derivation
also relies on a linearized loss change and a linearized product update, so the optimizer does not control
higher-order effects or the dropped ∆B∆A term. Finally, our evaluation focuses on LoRA finetuning for
language models and on held-out loss as the main measure of progress; broader downstream evaluations,
longer training horizons, other adapter families, and different hardware or batch-size regimes remain open.
These limitations point to several future directions. One is to design better outergradient approximations
that remain cheap enough for adapter training. Another is to incorporate second-order information [15]
while keeping the update tractable. More broadly, the per-sample loss-control perspective (8) and (12) may
be useful beyond LoRA, for pretraining or for other compositions of maps.
