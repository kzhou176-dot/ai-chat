Auditing Question-Order Effects in Large Language Models with the
QQ Equality: Mechanism Characterization and a Saturation Caveat
Pilsung Kang
Department of Software Science, Dankook University
Yongin, South Korea
pilsungk@dankook.ac.kr
Abstract
Human survey respondents exhibit question-order effects that satisfy the QQ (quantum
question) equality, an a priori, parameter-free prediction of the projective quantum question-
order model. We develop the QQ equality into an audit criterion for sequential judgments of
autoregressive large language models (LLMs). Theoretically, we characterize which mechanism
classes satisfy it robustly: marginal-independent kernels satisfy QQ iff all four mismatch transition
rates coincide (a class containing the 2D rank-1 projective model with a fixed measurement
pair under state variation); a polarity- and position-dependent repetition family is characterized
by an exact cross-symmetry condition with closed-form violations; QQ-satisfying behaviors are
closed under order-matched mixing; and the rank-2 Contextuality-by-Default criterion translates
into audit coordinates as |qQQ| ≤OSS, where OSS (the order-sensitivity score) totals the order
sensitivity of the two marginals. Methodologically, we develop a pre-specified, audit-logged
pipeline applicable to any model exposing next-token log-probabilities; it combines worst-case
robustness envelopes, sampling-consistency spot checks, full label counterbalancing, and a
saturation diagnostic. Empirically, in a first-signal pilot on an open-weight instruction-tuned
model under two framings, all pre-specified health gates passed, yet 17/18 and 7/8 item pairs,
respectively, were saturated (near-deterministic), and no item was certified residually contextual.
Forced-binary next-token log-probabilities were thus inadequate for distribution-level QQ audits
under the tested model and prompting conditions; we recommend pre-specified saturation
diagnostics whenever next-token distributions are treated as survey-response distributions.

Introduction
Question order changes human survey answers, sometimes dramatically. In a Gallup poll conducted
in September 1997, half of the respondents were asked whether Bill Clinton was honest and
trustworthy and then the same question about Al Gore; the other half answered the same two
questions in the opposite order. Asked first, Clinton received a 50% agreement rate and Gore 68% —
an 18-point gap; asked second, Clinton rose to 57% while Gore fell to 60%, shrinking the gap to 3
points [1, 2]. Order effects of this kind are pervasive and well documented, and by themselves they
are not surprising.
What is surprising is a sharper regularity hiding beneath them. Wang et al. [3] documented
that across 72 datasets, mostly national field studies (plus laboratory experiments), the two order-
conditioned joint distributions satisfy the QQ (quantum question) equality

question. This is an a priori, parameter-free prediction of the projective quantum question-order
model [2]. Equivalently, the probability that the two answers disagree is the same in both orders: in
the Clinton–Gore poll above, the disagreement probability was 0.221 in one order and 0.225 in the
other, even though the individual agreement rates moved by up to eight points. The equality is
thus a non-parametric constraint on the pair of joint distributions — not a no-order-effect claim —
and its empirical success is what makes it a candidate audit criterion rather than a curiosity.
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as proxies for human respondents, and
exhibit well-documented order and format sensitivities [4–6]. This raises a natural audit question:
does the sequential judgment behavior of an autoregressive LLM satisfy the same non-parametric
constraint that human populations do? To the best of our knowledge (within our 2026-07 search
scope; Section 2), no prior work has tested the QQ equality on LLMs under a multi-turn sequential
protocol with answers committed to the conversation history.
Testing this constraint on an LLM is not a direct transcription of the human protocol, because
the measured object differs. A human study estimates response distributions from repeated sampling
across respondents; an LLM study typically treats the model’s next-token probability distribution,
read out at a forced-choice answer position, as the response distribution of a synthetic respondent.
This substitution is convenient and widely used, but it is a measurement assumption, not a fact: the
constraint is informative only if the measured distribution actually carries distributional structure,
and nothing guarantees that an instruction-tuned model exposes such structure at a forced binary
choice. Making this assumption explicit, testable, and — where it fails — auditable is as much a
part of our task as the QQ test itself.
One caution governs the interpretation of any outcome. We deliberately use compatibility
language throughout: QQ satisfaction is consistent with projective measurement and with symmetric
classical repetition (Proposition 4); QQ violation rejects only the standard projective model, not
“quantum-like” accounts in general — quantum instruments based on positive operator-valued
measures (POVMs) can violate QQ [7].
Answering this question well therefore requires more than running a test. It requires knowing
what a result would mean (which mechanism classes are compatible with satisfaction or violation),
whether the measurement instrument is adequate, and what an inadequate instrument looks like in
practice. Our contributions track exactly these three needs:
1. Mechanism characterization (Section 3). We formalize mechanisms as maps from item
parameters to sequential response kernels and characterize robust QQ satisfaction in two
families (Theorem 3, Proposition 5), prove closure under order-matched mixing (Proposition 7),
exhibit a classical mechanism that satisfies QQ identically while producing order effects
(Proposition 4), and translate the rank-2 Contextuality-by-Default (CbD) criterion into audit
coordinates (Corollary 6). The result is a discriminant table that turns audit outcomes into
compatibility statements.
2. Audit methodology (Section 4). A pre-specified, audit-logged pipeline — applicable to any
model that exposes next-token log-probabilities and admits single-token answer labels — for
the binary-conditioned audit estimand P(y | y or n) under multi-turn sequential presentation,
with exact robustness-envelope decisions over unassigned probability mass, pre-specified
Clopper–Pearson spot checks, full label counterbalancing with semantic canonicalization, and
a saturation diagnostic.
3. A first-signal pilot and a measurement caveat (Section 5). On Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507
under two framings (direct evaluation; survey-respondent persona), all pre-specified health
checks pass, yet the response distributions are saturated (near-deterministic) for 17/18 and

flips; no item was certified residually contextual (a few were indeterminate at the certification

Related Work
Our work sits at the intersection of two literatures: the quantum-cognition line that produced the
QQ equality and its contextuality-theoretic refinements, and the language-model evaluation line
that treats LLMs as survey respondents and scrutinizes how their answers are measured.
QQ equality and quantum question-order models.
Wang and Busemeyer [2] derived the QQ
equality from projective sequential measurement and showed that standard Bayesian and Markov
accounts do not generally satisfy it; Wang et al. [3] confirmed it across 72 datasets. The Supporting
Information of [3] further shows that a repeat-choice model constrained to satisfy QQ predicts
context effects proportional to marginal differences — empirically rejected in humans (r = .04) —
and that an anchoring-adjustment model satisfies QQ iff its two order biases are equal; both facts
foreshadow our characterization results, which we state and prove at the mechanism level. Ozawa
and Khrennikov [8] unify order effects, response replicability, and the QQ equality with quantum
instruments; Lebedev and Khrennikov [7] show general POVMs can violate QQ.
Contextuality-by-Default.
For inconsistently connected systems, CbD [9] provides the correct
contextuality criterion; for rank-2 cyclic systems it reduces to a single inequality that we show equals
|qQQ| ≤OSS in audit coordinates (Corollary 6). Prior CbD analyses of question-order data interpret
human order effects as direct influences. Recent concurrent work applies CbD to generative LLMs in
other task families: Kumar et al. [10] report irreducible context-dependence in a pronoun-inference
setting, while Tuan [11] find zero corrected CbD degree in an enterprise-agent auditing pilot, with
direct influence as the dominant signal — consistent with our findings in the question-order setting.
Quantum-inspired tests on LLMs.
Imannezhad et al. [12] test conjunction/disjunction fallacies
and binary complementarity on GPT-5; Aerts et al. [13] report Bell-type violations and Bose–Einstein
fits in concept combinations (interpretation debated); Lo et al. [14] report large-scale sheaf- and
CbD-contextuality in BERT probability distributions over a co-reference-style linguistic schema.
None tests the QQ equality under sequential multi-turn presentation.
LLMs as survey respondents.
Tjuatja et al. [4] document human-like response biases; Rupprecht
et al. [5] test 18 LLMs on World Values Survey items under ten perturbations (334,800 interviews),
finding a consistent recency bias in almost all tested models; Sandhan et al. [6] evaluate LLM
personality tests with conversational context and report model-dependent sensitivity to question
ordering. Our results add a distribution-level caveat to this literature: under forced-binary formats
the “response distribution” of an instruction-tuned model can be an artifact of a saturated next-token
probability distribution, so distribution-level constructs (including QQ) require measurement designs
that restore genuine dispersion.
3

First-token measurement critique.
Wang et al. [15] show first-token probabilities can disagree
with generated text answers. Our saturation finding extends the critique from disagreement to
degeneracy: even when first-token measurement is internally consistent (our logprob and trajectory-
sampling pathways agree, in the pre-specified spot checks, to exact Clopper–Pearson standards),
the measured distribution may carry almost no information beyond its argmax.
3
Theory: Which Mechanisms Satisfy the QQ Equality?
QQ satisfaction alone does not identify a unique generating mechanism, and QQ violation alone does
not establish residual contextuality. This section therefore characterizes mechanism classes compati-
ble with satisfaction or violation and translates the rank-2 CbD criterion into audit coordinates,
providing the interpretive basis for the discriminant table and empirical analysis.
3.1
Setup
For binary questions A, B with answers α, β ∈{y, n}, define order-conditioned kernels pA(α), KAB(β | α)
and pB(β), KBA(α | β), with joints jointAB(α, β) = pA(α)KAB(β | α) (and symmetrically for BA).
The observable behavior space is 6-dimensional. Writing MXY for the probability that the two
answers disagree in order XY , qQQ = MAB −MBA.
Definition 1 (Mechanism; robust QQ). A mechanism maps item parameters θ = (pA, pB, . . . ) to
kernel pairs, with fixed internal parameters. It satisfies QQ robustly if qQQ = 0 on an admissible
domain containing an open subset of (0, 1)2 in the first-position marginals.
Finite experiments certify compatibility only, never robustness. We write the mismatch transition
rates as a = KAB(n|y), b = KAB(y|n), c = KBA(n|y), and d = KBA(y|n). Order effects on the
marginals are OEA = pBA(Ay) −pAB(Ay) and OEB = pAB(By) −pBA(By), the change in each
question’s “yes” marginal when it is asked second rather than first. We abbreviate q for qQQ where
unambiguous.
Throughout the paper, results are stated and proved in classical probability terms (response
kernels and transition rates); quantum models enter only as particular mechanism classes to be
included in or excluded from compatibility statements (e.g., Theorem 2), and no quantum formalism
is needed to follow the audit methodology or the empirical results.

Results
Theorem 2 (Wang–Busemeyer [2]). Projective sequential measurement satisfies qQQ = 0 for every
state and every pair of binary projective measurements.
We reproduce this numerically to machine precision in our validation suite.
Theorem 3 (Marginal-independent kernels). If (a, b, c, d) are constants independent of (pA, pB),
then with u = pA(y), v = pB(y),
qQQ = u(a −b) −v(c −d) + (b −d),
(2)
and robust QQ holds iff a = b = c = d.
Proof. Coefficient elimination on an open set.

polarity-invariant retention gives q = (rBA −rAB) S with S := pA(y)pB(n) + pA(n)pB(y) (position-
dependent repetition). These closed forms are pre-specified alternative explanations for violations.
Corollary 6 (CbD criterion in audit coordinates). For the rank-2 cyclic system with ±1 coding,
the Kujala–Dzhafarov noncontextuality criterion [9] is equivalent to
|qQQ| ≤OSS := |OEA| + |OEB|,
(4)
where we call OSS the order-sensitivity score of the item. Thus qQQ = 0 implies noncontextuality;
violations with |q| ≤OSS are compatible with a noncontextual coupling that allows direct influences
(the criterion does not identify direct influence as the generating cause); only |q| > OSS certifies
residual contextuality. OSS is thereby an inconsistent-connectedness allowance. (The mathematical
content is [9]; our contribution is the translation identity |E[AB]AB −E[AB]BA| = 2|qQQ|, ∆=
2 OSS.)
Proposition 7 (Order-matched mixing closure). qQQ is a linear functional of the joint pair, so any
mixture of QQ-satisfying behaviors with weights identical across the two orders (possibly θ-dependent)
satisfies QQ; order-dependent component selection can violate QQ (a possible violation channel, not
a necessity). This provides one sufficient route by which heterogeneous populations may preserve
QQ satisfaction in aggregate, is consistent with the pooled estimand used for counterbalanced label
mappings (Section 4), and marks order-dependent routing (e.g., in mixture-of-experts decoding) as
an audit checkpoint.
Two numerical facts are retained as conjecture-generating evidence: symmetric unsharp qubit
L¨uders instruments preserved QQ in all tested configurations, and violations were observed in some
tested asymmetric variants (consistent with the possibility results of [7]). Open problems include the
general characterization and representation question, Bayesian-update boundaries, and higher-rank
cyclic audits.

Audit Methodology
This section translates the theoretical distinctions above into a reproducible audit pipeline for
sequential judgments of LLMs.
The procedure defines the estimand and history-conditioned
branching protocol, controls label and unassigned-mass effects, and checks both computational
consistency and distributional adequacy before QQ or contextuality verdicts are interpreted.
Estimand.
The binary-conditioned audit estimand is P(y | y or n) = eℓy/(eℓy + eℓn), computed
from the next-token log-probabilities of the canonical single-token labels. Joint probabilities are
assembled as p(Aa, Bb) = P(Aa) P(Bb | history contains Aa) under a history-conditioned forced-
branch sequential protocol: the first question is asked, each candidate answer is committed to
the conversation history in turn, and the second question is asked conditional on that history, in
both orders. This estimand differs from full-vocabulary generation; the gap is tracked through the
recorded raw label mass at every position.
Label counterbalancing.
Original survey wordings and response options are preserved; options
map to neutral tokens A/B under both assignments (map-1, map-2), each applied identically across
orders and protocols. Results are canonicalized back to semantic options before pooling with equal
weights. Pooling is justified by the linearity of qQQ in the joint pair together with the pre-specified
equal-weight estimand; Proposition 7 additionally clarifies how such order-matched mixtures preserve
QQ structure. Per-mapping results are reported as label-effect diagnostics (the two mappings are
experimental conditions, not latent population components).
6

Decision rules (pre-specified; frozen before execution and audit-logged).
Unassigned
probability mass (“other”) is handled by a worst-case robustness envelope: qQQ is multilinear in the
six mass-allocation variables, so its exact range over the allocation box is attained on 26 vertices;
the equivalence decision (SATISFIED/VIOLATED/INDETERMINATE at εQQ = 0.02, with a
floating-point tolerance) is taken over the envelope. The margin εQQ was fixed a priori as a practical-
equivalence band wide enough to cover approximate satisfaction by partial-revision processes, for
which simulations in the Supporting Information of Wang et al. [3] report |q| < 0.015. Γ = |q| −OSS
is only piecewise multilinear, so certified bounds are assembled from exact component envelopes
(sound by elementary inequalities; conservatism verified empirically). Logprob and trajectory-level
rejection sampling are cross-checked by exact Clopper–Pearson intervals with Bonferroni correction
(per item-order, α = 0.01), a method selected after an observed simultaneous undercoverage of
Wilson intervals (0.980 vs 0.995 at n = 200 in calibration).
Saturation diagnostic (pre-specified).
An item is saturated if more than half of its measured
positions have max(p, 1 −p) > 0.99. Saturated items carry little distributional information; their
QQ verdicts are reported but weakly interpretable.
Health gates.
Gate G1 requires the canonical labels to be single tokens in the rendered chat-
template context. Gate G2 requires the other mass to stay below 10−2 at every position, screening
gross mass leakage; the decision-relevant quantity is the resulting envelope width, which is reported
with the results. Gate G3 cross-checks logprob against sampling as above, run as pre-specified spot
checks rather than exhaustively: under Track M, items s1-01 and s3-01, both orders, nrej = 200
accepted joint samples each; under Track P, item s1-01, both orders, nrej = 200.
The mathematical core was validated against ground-truth agents (projective, repetition families,
L¨uders instruments), recovering q = 0 at machine precision and all closed-form violations exactly;
the full software assertion suite ships with the code as part of the reproducibility materials.
5
Empirical Pilot
This section applies the audit pipeline to a first-signal pilot on an instruction-tuned LLM, evaluating
both the technical reliability of the procedure and whether the resulting response distributions are
adequate for meaningful QQ and CbD analysis.
Setup.
All measurements were taken on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 [16], run in bfloat16 (bf16) in
non-thinking mode; this is a single-model study, and no cross-model generalization is claimed (see
Limitations). The item set consists of 18 question pairs: 10 historical survey pairs with original
wordings and response options, drawn from Wang et al. [3] and related classic survey sources (Gallup,
Pew, SRC) and forming stratum S1; 3 name-swapped memorization controls (S2); and 5 novel
judgment pairs answered with Yes/No labels (S3). Two framings were tested, labeled Track M and
Track P, keeping the names used in the pre-specified protocol and its audit records. Track M applies
a direct evaluation instruction; Track P, run once as a redesign rerun frozen before execution on the
8 year-fixed S1/S2 pairs, applies a survey-respondent persona with a per-item reference year. We
use M and P as shorthand in tables and figures; the full prompts and the item manifest are given in
Appendix B. Measurement was deterministic at the logprob level, with decoding for the sampling
checks fixed at temperature 1 and top-p 1. Every run writes an immutable audit record containing
the model and tokenizer revisions, the chat-template hash, label token ids, mapping assignment,
seeds, and rejection attempt and accept counts.

Main finding (methodological).
Under the tested instruction-tuned model and prompting
conditions, forced-binary next-token log-probabilities (i) revealed substantial order and label sen-
sitivity — the deterministic flips and label-mapping effects above are observed properties of the
binary-conditioned estimand; but were (ii) inadequate for measuring population-like dispersed re-

6
Discussion
This section interprets the pilot findings in relation to LLM-as-respondent research, clarifies what
they imply for QQ and CbD audits, and outlines measurement designs that may yield more
informative response distributions.
Relation to LLM-as-respondent studies.
Studies that aggregate LLM “response distributions”
from forced-choice next-token measurements implicitly assume dispersion. Our results show that for
a modern instruction-tuned model this assumption can fail for nearly all tested items at the single-
policy level, with apparent distributional effects driven by deterministic flips and label-assignment
effects. This sharpens, at the distribution level, the first-token critique of Wang et al. [15].
What a QQ audit needs instead.
The theory clarifies both the opportunity and a hazard.
Human aggregates are mixtures of individuals; if individual-level behavior satisfies QQ, Proposition 7
explains aggregate satisfaction under order-matched mixing — but the converse direction matters
for design: mixture populations (personas; temperature ensembles with order-invariant selection)
and alternative measurement designs (base checkpoints, sampling-based estimands) may restore
genuine dispersion; mixtures in particular can also average away component-level violations, so a
mixture-based audit must report component-level diagnostics alongside aggregate verdicts.
What the pilot does and does not show.
This pilot documents a measurement caveat
within a deliberately narrow empirical scope. Its evidence comes from one instruction-tuned model
11

and 18 item pairs, many drawn from U.S. political and social survey contexts, under a single
binary-conditioned next-token estimand and two prompt framings. Accordingly, the persistence
of saturation should be interpreted as a property of the tested model–prompt–item configuration,
not as evidence that instruction-tuned LLMs generally produce saturated response distributions.
What the pilot does show is that, under these conditions, a technically healthy and internally
consistent audit can still be limited by near-deterministic outputs; it does not establish whether the
same limitation persists across model scales, base checkpoints, languages, cultures, or broader item
domains.
In addition, the pilot does not show that LLMs are, or are not, “quantum-like”. QQ satisfaction is
compatible with classical repetition (Proposition 4), QQ violation is compatible with noncontextual
direct influences (Corollary 6), and in the saturated regime many verdicts are dominated by
deterministic or label-sensitive behavior; the informative content of the pilot therefore concerns the
measurement layer more than the mechanism layer. The absence of certified contextuality here is
consistent with the concurrent enterprise-agent audit of Tuan [11], where direct influence dominated
the signal, but contrasts with the irreducible context-dependence reported by Kumar et al. [10] in a
pronoun-inference setting. Task family, estimand construction, and the dispersion of the underlying
distributions all differ across these studies, and reconciling the two regimes is a natural target for
the distribution-restoring designs above.
For the audit engine.
The components that remain useful beyond this pilot are the character-
ization theorems, the discriminant table, and the validated pipeline with its health gates; these
contributions do not depend on the particular LLM tested here. In an audit workflow, the saturation
diagnostic serves as a screening check for whether a system’s judgment interface exposes sufficient
distributional structure for downstream analysis.
7
Limitations
The empirical scope of this study is deliberately narrow. The reported results come from a single
instruction-tuned model, with Track M covering 18 English-language item pairs and Track P covering
the eight year-fixed S1/S2 pairs under a respondent-persona framing. They therefore do not support
cross-model, cross-linguistic, or cross-cultural generalization. Many items were drawn from U.S.
political and social survey traditions, although the set also included matched name-substitution
controls and newly constructed judgment pairs. We further examined only one measurement scheme:
binary-conditioned next-token log-probabilities obtained through single-token forced-choice labels.
The use of neutral A/B labels with an explicit per-turn legend is itself part of this design, and other
response formats, model scales, base checkpoints, or estimands may produce different patterns of
label sensitivity, dispersion, and saturation. Accordingly, our empirical conclusion is limited to the
persistence of saturation under the tested model–prompt–item–estimand configurations, rather than
to instruction-tuned LLMs or next-token measurement in general.
The inferential and interpretive scope is also limited. Two Track M items and three Track P
items remained Γ-indeterminate at the certification bounds. Our envelope procedure certifies
noncontextuality when the certified upper bound on Γ is nonpositive. For the indeterminate items,
however, failure to certify residual contextuality should not be interpreted as evidence that it is
absent. Human joint-probability benchmarks were unavailable for direct calibration, so comparisons
with historical survey findings remain qualitative. More fundamentally, QQ satisfaction establishes
compatibility with multiple mechanism classes rather than identifying a unique generating mechanism
(Proposition 4). Conversely, a QQ violation shows that the measured binary-conditioned joint
12

distributions are incompatible with the standard projective question-order model; it does not identify
the model’s internal mechanism or exclude more general quantum-like instrument models [7]. Finally,
the claim that no prior study has tested the QQ equality on LLMs under a committed, multi-turn
sequential protocol is limited to the literature identified within our July 2026 search window.
8
Conclusion
We developed the QQ equality—an a priori constraint of the standard projective quantum question-
order model that has been observed across human survey data—into an audit criterion for sequential
binary judgments of autoregressive LLMs. By characterizing robust QQ satisfaction in several
mechanism families, establishing closure under order-matched mixing, and translating the rank-2
CbD criterion into the audit inequality |qQQ| ≤OSS, we clarified what inferential claims QQ
satisfaction and violation can and cannot support.
In the empirical pilot, all pre-specified health gates passed, indicating that the sequential
branching, label-token measurement, robustness envelopes, and sampling spot checks operated as
intended. Nevertheless, the binary-conditioned distributions were saturated for 17 of 18 Track M
items and 7 of 8 Track P items, while counterbalanced mappings showed that neutral-label assignment
could materially change the measured qQQ and OSS. The pooled audit produced both QQ satisfaction
and violation, but no item had a certified Γlower > 0; many violations instead lay near the boundary
|qQQ| = OSS and were therefore compatible with noncontextual couplings that allow between-order
marginal changes, with several items remaining indeterminate at the certification bounds. These
findings do not establish whether the model is or is not “quantum-like.” Rather, they show that,
under the tested model–prompt–item conditions, a technically reproducible forced-binary next-token
estimand can remain too nearly deterministic to support fine-grained mechanism discrimination.
Methodologically, the pilot shows that distribution-level audits of LLMs should assess the
adequacy of the response distribution before interpreting higher-level structural criteria. Saturation
diagnostics, label counterbalancing, and explicit tracking of unassigned probability mass should
therefore accompany any use of next-token probabilities as survey-response distributions. Future
work should test whether this measurement caveat persists across broader model families and
alternative measurement designs, while retaining component-level diagnostics whenever mixture-
based approaches are used to restore dispersion.

map-1:
option 1 7→A,
option 2 7→B,
map-2:
option 1 7→B,
option 2 7→A.

coordinates. The two mappings were then pooled with equal, pre-specified weights. Per-mapping

B.4
Representative verbatim items
The following examples illustrate the three item strata and the different response formats. Question
wording and semantic options are reproduced verbatim from the frozen item file.
Example 1: original survey item with non-Yes/No options (s1-04)
Question A.
Do you think that only a few or many white people dislike black people?
Question B.
Do you think that only a few or many black people dislike white people?
Semantic options: A few and Many. These options were mapped to A/B under both counterbalanced
mappings. The item was marked sensitive and used reference year 1996 in Track P.
Example 2: matched name-substitution item (s2-01)
Question A.
Do you generally think Tony Blair is honest and trustworthy?
20

Question B.
Do you generally think Gordon Brown is honest and trustworthy?
Semantic options: Yes and No. The item was matched to s1-01 and used reference year 1997 in
Track P.
Example 3: novel vignette item (s3-01)
Vignette.
Two candidates applied for the same software engineering role. Candidate K has strong
qualifications but a two-year employment gap. Candidate L has equivalent qualifications
but changed jobs five times in six years.
Question A.
Should Candidate K advance to the interview stage?
Question B.
Should Candidate L advance to the interview stage?
The canonical responses for this stratum were Yes and No; no A/B remapping was applied.
